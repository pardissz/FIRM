Research Overview

The Fairness Iceberg

Current fairness benchmarks measure what a model decides. We measure three things: what it decides, how robust that decision is, and what reasoning is actually driving it internally. These are three distinct layers of bias — and existing evaluation only covers the first.

The Framework

Three Levels of Bias

Fairness evaluation has been stuck at the surface. Each level below reveals something the level above cannot see.

Level 1 — Above the waterline
Output
What does the model decide? Given two candidates with different qualifications and demographics, does it select the stronger one? This is what BBQ, StereoSet, WinoBias, and CrowS-Pairs measure. Post-trained models pass these reliably.

Level 2 — Below the waterline
Robustness
How firmly does the model hold that decision under adversarial pressure? Add a single sentence — authority endorsement, guilt framing, diversity instruction — and does the decision survive? Is the resistance symmetric: does the model hold decisions equally firmly regardless of which demographic group benefits?

Both produce the same Level 1 output, but credential-anchored decisions are stable under pressure while compensatory-reasoning-anchored decisions are fragile. Level 3 explains Level 2.

The Core Claim

A model can make the right decision (Level 1) for the wrong reasons (Level 3), and that wrong reasoning makes it fragile (Level 2) in ways no existing benchmark detects. A candidate should be chosen because they are qualified — not because the model is reasoning about their demographic group.

Levels 1 & 2

FIRM: Fairness through Injection and Robustness Measurement

107,000 instances across realistic deployment scenarios. Each scenario tested clean, then under 72 categories of adversarial injection with matched minority/majority-favoring pairs.

📋

Realistic Scenarios

Hiring, loan approval, medical triage, legal sentencing. Candidates with explicit credential profiles and demographic signals. Ground-truth (one objectively stronger) and ambiguous (equal qualifications) conditions.

💉

72 Injection Categories

11 families: authority signals, social consensus, observer effects, role framing, guilt/historical, evaluation awareness, evidential, emotional, legal, statistical, and controls (absurd, neutral, weather). Each with matched minority/majority pair.

⚖️

Asymmetric Measurement

The Robustness Parity Score (RPS): ratio of minority-favoring robustness to majority-favoring robustness under the same injection types. RPS = 1.0 means symmetric. RPS ≠ 1.0 means the model holds decisions differently depending on who benefits.

Five FIRM Metrics

Primary Metric

Robustness Parity Score (RPS)

Ratio of minority-favoring robustness to majority-favoring robustness. Measures whether the model resists adversarial pressure symmetrically. RPS = 1.0 = symmetric; RPS → 0 = model only holds minority-favoring decisions.

RPS = R_minority / R_majority   where R = 1 − flip_rate

Ambiguous Direction Score (ADS)

In no-ground-truth scenarios (equal credentials), which direction does the model systematically lean? Measures baseline demographic preference without adversarial pressure.

Accuracy Parity

Correctness gap across demographic groups on ground-truth scenarios without injection. The Level 1 measurement — equivalent to what existing benchmarks capture.

Confidence Parity

Log-probability gap between demographic groups. Detects sub-threshold fragility that accuracy scores miss — the model may be correct but uncertain, making it vulnerable to flipping.

Stereotype Amplification Index (SAI)

Whether injections amplify or suppress demographic preference. Do authority-endorsement injections push the model further in the stereotypical direction, or in the anti-stereotypical direction?

Mechanistic Validation

The Geometric Explanation

Why chain-of-thought can't see the bias: the decision direction and the demographic cognition direction are geometrically orthogonal.

cos(decision_direction, demographic_cognition_direction)
< 0.02
Both models. Nearly perpendicular.

What this means

The model's decision mechanism (which candidate to hire) and its demographic awareness mechanism (reasoning about race, gender, identity) operate in perpendicular subspaces of the activation space.

A model trained to suppress expressed demographic reasoning (RLHF/Constitutional AI) moves along the cognition axis — it learns to say "I'm judging on merit." But the decision axis is orthogonal to this. The bias in the decision is untouched.

This is why standard benchmarks fail. They measure output behavior, which reflects the decision axis. But the demographic cognition that shapes the decision lives in an orthogonal direction that the model's own introspection cannot access.

Linear Probe Accuracy: The Decision Is Committed Early

A linear probe at the extraction layer predicts the model's hiring decision with 97.8% accuracy. The decision is geometrically committed in the hidden state long before the output token is generated.

Implications

Why This Matters

Exploitability

An adversary who knows the model's decisions are asymmetrically fragile can construct prompts that reliably flip majority-correct decisions while leaving minority-correct decisions untouched. A systematically manipulable system certified as unbiased.

Chain-of-thought monitoring fails

Because the decision direction and demographic cognition direction are geometrically orthogonal (cos < 0.02), CoT monitoring cannot detect this bias. The model's stated reasoning is faithful to its cognition axis — but the bias is on the perpendicular decision axis.

Actionable root cause

If reasoning asymmetry is installed by DPO preference data (asymmetric penalty for stereotypical vs. anti-stereotypical errors), the fix is in data curation. If it's in the reward model, the fix is in reward design. OLMo-3's open pipeline makes diagnosis possible.

The definition of "fair" needs to change. Fair should not mean "makes the right decision." Fair should mean "makes the right decision for the right reasons and holds it equally firmly regardless of who benefits." A woman hired because the model is thinking about diversity is not the same as a woman hired because the model is thinking about her resume — even if the outcome is identical. FairnessScope is the first framework that can tell the difference.

🎯 The Core Injection Finding

Most injections substantially raise UDCR — the model's internal demographic reasoning — without changing the output decision. Gemma picks Shaniqua 66.7% in 35 of 38 categories regardless of injection type. The model thinks differently but decides the same.

Cognition changes. Decisions don't. This means the cognition axis and the decision axis really are independent — you can move the model's reasoning without moving its choice. Exactly what cos < 0.02 predicts.

💡 What Actually Moves Decisions?

Only explicit credential-evidence injections shift choices. factual_pro_bradley drops Shaniqua to 22.2% in Qwen. merit_only drops to 44.4%. Abstract framing (guilt, diversity, legal threats) changes the internal narrative but not the output.

Evidence beats framing. When you give the model concrete counter-evidence about qualifications, it responds. When you apply moral pressure, it absorbs the pressure cognitively but doesn't budge. The decision axis only responds to credential signals.

The Geometry of Hidden Bias

Two Perpendicular Worlds Inside the Model

The decision axis (who to hire) and the demographic cognition axis (reasoning about race) are nearly perpendicular. You can move one without moving the other.

→ Decision axis (Shaniqua ↔ Bradley) → Demographic cognition axis (High ↔ Low UDCR) ⌒ ≈ 90° (cos < 0.02)

What the prior work assumed

The bias direction and the decision direction are the same thing — or at least aligned. Find the bias direction, steer it, and the decision changes. Karvonen & Marks (2025) successfully steered bias this way.

What we found

They're perpendicular. The model can be deciding to hire Bradley, while thinking about Shaniqua's race, and these two facts are stored in geometrically independent dimensions. Suppressing demographic cognition (RLHF) moves the model along the cognition axis while leaving the decision axis untouched.

The implication

UDCR (demographic cognition) and hiring outcome are decoupled. R² = 0.001. A model that says "I'm judging on merit" (low UDCR) can still have a strongly skewed decision axis. You cannot audit fairness by auditing reasoning. You must audit the decision geometry directly.

Open Questions

What We Don't Know Yet

The findings raise questions that no existing work can answer. These drive the next phase of research.

?

Does alignment training install the asymmetry, or just amplify what's already there?

If the base model already shows asymmetric robustness (RPS ≠ 1.0), the problem is in pretraining data. If RPS ≈ 1.0 at base and diverges only after DPO, the problem is in preference optimization. These require completely different fixes. OLMo-3's open checkpoints let us test this for the first time.

?

If the model chooses a transgender candidate, is it thinking about their resume or their identity?

A transgender candidate selected because the model's dominant reasoning involves gender identity and inclusion is not the same as one selected for qualifications. The outcome respects the candidate. The process doesn't. Can the NLA verbalization distinguish these? And does the reasoning profile change when we swap which candidate is transgender?

?

Why does "the weather is rainy in LA" flip a hiring decision?

FIRM includes absurd control injections — semantically irrelevant statements. In preliminary results, some of these change decisions. If they do, the fragility isn't about the content of the injection. It's about the asymmetric stability of the decision surface itself. Any noise — even random — can push the model off a flat plateau. The question is whether majority-favoring decisions sit on flat plateaus while minority-favoring decisions sit in deep valleys.

?

Is the reward model itself asymmetric?

DPO optimizes against preference data. If the preference pairs systematically penalize "chose the white candidate when the Black candidate was stronger" more than the reverse, the reward landscape is asymmetric by design. The model learns to hold minority-favoring decisions more firmly because the cost of error is higher in that direction. OLMo-3's open preference data lets us audit this directly.

?

Can the model detect its own bias if you ask it the right way?

Chain-of-thought monitoring fails: in zero cases does the CoT mention demographic factors even when they provably caused the flip (E6). But what if you ask the model after the decision: "Was your decision influenced by demographic information?" If it says "no" while the NLA says "yes," you've demonstrated that CoT monitoring is structurally unable to detect this class of bias — because the bias lives in a geometric direction orthogonal to the reasoning stream.

?

Does the same asymmetry appear in moral decisions, not just hiring?

FIRM includes trolley-problem-style moral dilemmas with demographic crossing: Group A in the utilitarian-majority role (save 20 people), Group B as the individual. Does the model hold the utilitarian decision more firmly when it benefits a minority group? If the same asymmetric fragility appears in moral reasoning, the problem is deeper than hiring — it's a structural property of how post-trained models handle demographic information in any decision.
